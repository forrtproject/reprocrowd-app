[sec1.p6.s1] AFFECTIVE EXPECTATIONS REPLICATION

[sec1.p7.s1] 2 Abstract According to the Affective Expectation Model (AEM), people’s evaluations of experiences depend on their expectations.
[sec1.p7.s2] This generally happens in an assimilative way (positive expectations lead to positive experiences) unless there is a strong discrepancy between the expectation and the experience.
[sec1.p7.s3] In this case, a contrast effect occurs (positive expectations lead to negative experiences).
[sec1.p7.s4] We conducted an online replication of Study 1 from Geers and Lassiter (2003) where affective experiences were positively correlated with affective expectations for people with a low need for cognition and negatively correlated for people with a high need for cognition.
[sec1.p7.s5] None of the original effects could be replicated, that is, there was no evidence for a difference between the groups nor for a moderation of affective experiences by need for cognition (η²original = .04, 90% CI [.005, .10] , η²replication < 0.01, 90% CI [0, 0.02]).
[sec1.p7.s6] We discuss potential limitations of the AEM.
[sec1.p7.s7] The study’s preregistration, data, and materials are available online (https://osf.io/rge23/).
[sec1.p7.s8] Keywords: affective expectations, replication, assimilation and contrast, need for cognition

[sec1.p9.s1] 3 No Evidence for the Affective Expectation Model: Replication of Geers and Lassiter (2003) “Life is like a box of chocolate – you never know what you are going to get” (Zemeckis, 1994).
[sec1.p9.s2] Forrest Gump leads us through the film named after him with his life philosophy, which sticks to him and his experiences throughout the film.
[sec1.p9.s3] While we will never know what will await us, we often form expectations of events which have not yet taken place and lie in the future.
[sec1.p9.s4] Although expectations are often formed spontaneously, we have some control over what to expect from a certain situation.
[sec1.p9.s5] For example, big expectations could not be fulfilled and lead to a feeling of disappointment, following an adjustment of expectations to avoid a repetition of said feeling.
[sec1.p9.s6] But is this really the best coping strategy?
[sec1.p9.s7] According to the Affective Expectation Model (AEM), this might not be the case.
[sec1.p9.s8] Here, expectations are defined as “people’s predictions about how they will feel in a particular situation or toward a specific stimulus” (Wilson & Klaaren, 1992, p.
[sec1.p9.s9] 3).
[sec1.p9.s10] According to the AEM “affect and emotion are often formed in an expectation- driven fashion” (Wilson et al., 1989).
[sec1.p9.s11] Thus, the affective evaluation of an event (i.e., how sad or funny we rate something) does not only depend on its quality but also our affective expectations (i.e., how sad or funny we expect to rate something).
[sec1.p9.s12] Generally, affective expectations are positively associated with affective experiences (assimilation effect: more positive affective expectations lead to a more positive affective experience) unless the affective expectation of a stimulus and its actual valence are incongruent and the incongruence is noticed (contrast effect: more positive affective expectations lead to a less positive affective experiences).
[sec1.p9.s13] Geers and Lassiter demonstrated assimilation and contrast effects by inducing a finer unitization of information regarding the stimulus and therefore enhancing the likeliness to detect an incongruence (Geers & Lassiter, 1999) and by including participants’ optimism (Geers & Lassiter, 2002) and need for cognition (Geers & Lassiter, 2003) in the model.

[sec1.p11.s2] Choice of target study We replicated Study 1 from Geers and Lassiter (2003) to re-test the hypothesis that participants with a high need for cognition (NFC) are more likely to notice an incongruence between expectation and experience and therefore produce contrast effects.
[sec1.p11.s3] The concept of need for cognition is understood as “the tendency for an individual to engage in and enjoy thinking” (Cacioppo & Petty, 1982, p.116).
[sec1.p11.s4] Especially the tendency to “seek, acquire, think about, and reflect back on information to make sense of stimuli, relationships, and events in their world” (Cacioppo & Petty, 1982, p.
[sec1.p11.s5] 198) is what enhances the probability of a finer, more pronounced elaboration of information and therefore could influence one’s capability to detect an incongruence of expectation-stimulus relation.
[sec1.p11.s6] Individuals with high need for cognition scores thus evaluate messages more strongly in dependence of their quality (Cacioppo et al., 1983), are more likely to have an information-oriented identity style (Berzonsky & Sullivan, 1992) and divide experiences into more meaningful actions and therefore memorize them better (Lassiter et al., 1991) than individuals low in need for cognition.
[sec1.p11.s7] The tendency of persons with a high need for cognition to be “more likely to scrutinize their environment than individuals low in need for cognition” (Geers & Lassiter, 2003, p.
[sec1.p11.s8] 315) should lead to a contrast effect of expectations on experiences if expectations and experiences do not match.
[sec1.p11.s9] We chose to replicate an experiment that includes assimilation and contrast due to its role in research application: Creating and managing affective expectations is an important skill in politics, management, or marketing.

[sec1.p77.s1] 26 Discussion and Limitations In our replication of Geers and Lassiter (2003) Study 1, we could not find an effect of affective expectations on affective experience nor a moderation of that effect by participants’ need for cognition.
[sec1.p77.s2] It remains unclear whether the affective expectation model or the derived hypotheses are wrong or whether the study quality (e.g., statistical power, unclear efficacy of the manipulation) was insufficient to detect the effects.
[sec1.p77.s3] Most importantly, a lack of statistical power (~80%) may have led to a non-significant finding (false-negative) though our sample size is considerably larger than the original (Nreplication= 252, Noriginal = 160).
[sec1.p77.s4] We also did not conduct a strict manipulation check.
[sec1.p77.s5] For example, creating the materials from scratch and not piloting the induction (whether people’s expectations actually change) may have led to a non-working manipulation.
[sec1.p77.s6] However, we could show that participants remembered expectations were in line with the manipulation.
[sec1.p77.s7] It is also unlikely that this difference was due to a hindsight bias as it did not affect the affective experience.
[sec1.p77.s8] Another interpretation is that the manipulation did work but it was too weak.
[sec1.p77.s9] Making the evaluations in the expectation induction movies even more extreme would put their credibility at risk but may be necessary to produce the effects predicted by the AEM.
[sec1.p77.s10] The movie may also not be well suited for different types of expectations.
[sec1.p77.s11] The affect index was skewed towards negative ratings.
[sec1.p77.s12] However, variance of the affect index was large and with a generally negatively rated movie, main effects of affective expectations should have been detectable.
[sec1.p77.s13] There has been little systematic research on whether switching to online for a replication study affects replication outcomes with the only evidence known to us revealing a small and imprecise negative effect (Boyce et al., 2023, Table 2).
[sec1.p77.s14] Together with a sample size that was

[sec1.p78.s1] AFFECTIVE EXPECTATIONS REPLICATION

[sec1.p79.s1] 27 only about 1.5 times larger than the original study, statistical power may still have been insufficient.
[sec1.p79.s2] While attentiveness of participants was high in our replication, the cover story may not have worked.
[sec1.p79.s3] To test this, we recommend future studies to ask participants if they would indeed be willing to record their opinion on the movie and ask for contact details.
[sec1.p79.s4] Finally, in the light of field-wide replication failures (e.g., Camerer et al., 2018; Open Science Collaboration, 2015), strong and persistent publication biases (e.g., Giner-Sorolla, 2012; Sterling, 1959), and imprecise effect size estimates (e.g., the p-value for the positive slope in the regression model was not significant based on the reported and rounded values), it is possible that effects that we tried to replicate were false positives.
[sec1.p79.s5] Again, we do not believe that our results warrant this conclusion and that it is unclear whether our study simply failed to detect the effect or whether the effects are not robust.
[sec1.p79.s6] Deviations from the Preregistration Sample size.
[sec1.p79.s7] We did not achieve our preregistered target sample size for N = 400 participants but only N = 252, which still had >80% power for the original effect.
[sec1.p79.s8] Replication effects are usually about 40-50% the size of original effects (Röseler et al., 2024) and the replication may thus have been underpowered.
[sec1.p79.s9] Changes in the analysis code.
[sec1.p79.s10] We made changes to the preregistered analysis code (https://osf.io/6ua4s) that we highlighted with “ADDED” or “CHANGED” in the final analysis code (https://osf.io/2m3w6).
[sec1.p79.s11] These changes only included aesthetic changes or fixing of erroneous code.

[sec1.p80.s1] Conclusion

[sec1.p81.s1] AFFECTIVE EXPECTATIONS REPLICATION

[sec1.p82.s1] 28 Given our failure to replicate the effects of affective expectation on ratings of a film, we believe that the generalizability and robustness of the finding from Geers and Lassiter (2003) Study 1 is limited.
[sec1.p82.s2] We believe further research is necessary to determine effect sizes that are practically relevant and detectable, conditions for manipulations to work (e.g., about the expectation induction) and specific recommendations for stimuli with which the effects occur.
[sec1.p82.s3] Moreover, researchers need to describe the precise background conditions that are necessary for assimilation and contrast patterns to arise or define the threshold when an assimilation effect turns into a contrast effect.
[sec1.p82.s4] For example, we suggest the creation of a formal model (e.g., Robinaugh et al., 2021) that incorporates stimuli requirements such as whether the target movie should be ambivalent or not, and describes the relationship between expectations, experiences, and the tipping point (or region) between assimilation and contrast.
[sec1.p82.s5] While, overall, there is little research revisiting the role of affective expectations in the laboratory and affective expectations were unrelated to affective experiences in this study, Röseler and Schütz (2021) could show that people who watch a movie early or play a game early after its release do so because they have more positive expectations and also rate it more positively (assimilation effect).
[sec1.p82.s6] We thus see high potential for the AEM in relation to affective expectations, other fields such as placebo and nocebo effects, confirmation biases, the subject of assimilation and contrast in general (e.g., Röseler & Schütz, 2024), and its application in fields such as of health behavior, interpersonal relationships, or consumer behavior.
[sec1.p82.s7] We believe that conducting research on the Affective Expectation Model requires significant resources.
[sec1.p82.s8] Ideally, future research should use a controlled laboratory setting closer to the original study, create new and credible materials (e.g., like in the original study and in our online version, the expectation induction film should be set in an environment that is similar to

[sec1.p83.s1] AFFECTIVE EXPECTATIONS REPLICATION

[sec1.p84.s1] 29 the environment of the participants), conduct thorough pilot tests on the expectation induction and the target movie, use a freely available and non-copyrighted movie that should not need to be translated, include manipulation checks of the induction method, test for demand characteristics, and use a very large sample size (N ≥ 400).
[sec1.p84.s2] Obviously, it should adhere to modern standards of open science (e.g., preregistered methods, hypotheses, and analysis script, open materials, open access) to facilitate reproduction and replication.
[sec1.p84.s3] We hope that our study and materials will help to explore the limits of the AEM and encourage further replication attempts of studies on assimilation and contrast effects.
