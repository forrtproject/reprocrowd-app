[sec1.p2.s1] PERCEPTION OF GUMMY BEARS 2 Abstract People routinely perceive and evaluate things that are not alive, yet research on those perceptions and evaluations has focused on the level of the category (e.g., perceiving flowers, wine, food, artwork).
[sec1.p2.s2] Here we translate research on interpersonal perception to abiotic individuals and investigate individual differences between human perceivers in the accurate perception of and their unique preferences for gummy bear individuals of different flavors.
[sec1.p2.s3] Four members of one family, aged 8 to 47, each tasted 25 gummy bears drawn blind (100 trials in total).
[sec1.p2.s4] On each trial, the participant named the flavor among six known alternatives (strawberry, orange, apple, raspberry, lemon, pineapple), rated their certainty, and indicated their liking for the gummy bear.
[sec1.p2.s5] Liking for each flavor had also been stated before the tasting trials.
[sec1.p2.s6] Average accuracy in gummy bear identification (42%) was well above chance level (16.7%), with large interindividual variability (individual accuracies ranged from 12% to 68%).
[sec1.p2.s7] While stated liking was largely consensual between the family members, tasted liking was driven by the unique perceiver x flavor pairing.
[sec1.p2.s8] Also, stated preferences did not track tasted ones on average, and the correspondence varied widely across the four perceivers.
[sec1.p2.s9] Results underline the existence of individual differences in accurate perceptions of and unique preferences for gummy bears and point to fruitful avenues for future research on the perception of abiotic individuals in general.
[sec1.p2.s10] Keywords: interpersonal perception, individual differences, flavor identification, accuracy, uniqueness, gummy bears

[sec1.p3.s1] PERCEPTION OF GUMMY BEARS 3 Statement of Relevance People spend a great deal of their lives evaluating things that are not people: foods, products, objects, media.
[sec1.p3.s2] Psychology usually measures these evaluations at the level of the category, by asking which flavors, brands, or designs a person prefers.
[sec1.p3.s3] We measured what happens one level down, when a person meets a single member of a category without knowing which category it belongs to, in this case a gummy bear of unknown flavor placed directly in the mouth.
[sec1.p3.s4] What our participants said about the flavors in advance agreed well across family members, but the two flavors they named as best beforehand were not the two they liked best in the mouth.
[sec1.p3.s5] How well they could identify what they were eating also differed sharply from person to person.
[sec1.p3.s6] Two of the four could name the gummy bear flavor in their mouth far more often than guessing would allow, and two were found to be bad gummy bear judges.
[sec1.p3.s7] Surveys, menus, gift choices, and reward schemes for children all assume that a stated preference for a category describes the experience of its members.
[sec1.p3.s8] Measuring that experience directly is feasible, and it gives a different answer.

[sec1.p24.s1] PERCEPTION OF GUMMY BEARS 24 Discussion Our initial exploration of individual gummy bear perception revealed novel findings that might be taken up in future research.
[sec1.p24.s2] Most generally, across all three investigated questions, the gummy bears alone contributed remarkably little to their own perception.
[sec1.p24.s3] Instead, flavor identification, liking, and awareness were revealed in the mouth of the perceivers, the gummy bear eaters.
[sec1.p24.s4] How accurately a gummy bear was identified was mainly a property of the perceiver (i.e., the gummy bear eater), ranging from strong to chance-level accuracy.
[sec1.p24.s5] Some accurately identified flavors on most trials, while others rarely did so.
[sec1.p24.s6] How much a gummy bear was liked was a property of the gummy bear eater and flavor together.
[sec1.p24.s7] Ella specifically liked pineapple and liked apple less, while Mitja liked raspberry most and lemon less.
[sec1.p24.s8] How aware gummy bear eaters were of their specific gummy bear preferences differed across perceivers, with the correspondence between stated and tasted unique liking running from .13 to .61 and none of the four values differing from zero.
[sec1.p24.s9] There was no evidence for awareness on average.
[sec1.p24.s10] Future research across a larger set of perceivers is needed for a fuller examination of the degree of awareness and individual differences therein.
[sec1.p24.s11] Concerning accuracy, results differ markedly from those in related research areas.
[sec1.p24.s12] The average level of accuracy was below that found in cued identification research (also see Table S14).
[sec1.p24.s13] Each trial was a single retronasal encounter during chewing, the aroma inside a matrix whose acidity and texture do not distinguish the flavors.
[sec1.p24.s14] Despite name availability and feedback, two of the keys to odor identification (Cain, 1979), this task was more difficult than sniffing a labeled pen in typical cued identification paradigms.
[sec1.p24.s15] This finding could be due both to a less distinct sensory input and to a stronger influence of individual preconceptions regarding the experiences when interacting with gummy bears of a certain color that could interfere with the tasting task.
[sec1.p24.s16] At the same time, accuracy was above the level typically found in zero- or short-term acquaintance interpersonal perception research.
[sec1.p24.s17] So, once the differing

[sec1.p25.s1] PERCEPTION OF GUMMY BEARS 25 numbers of response alternatives are taken into account, identifying a gummy bear flavor in the mouth was easier than reading extraversion off a stranger's face (Jaeger et al., 2024), detecting lies (Bond & DePaulo, 2006), distinguishing a spontaneous from a posed smile (Perusquía-Hernández et al., 2019), or deciding whether a human face was real or a deepfake (Stockner et al., 2026).
[sec1.p25.s2] Again, this could be due to a more identifiable and less ambiguous set of sensory inputs (cues) as well as an even stronger role of preconceptions in the case of perceiving other living individuals as opposed to abiotic individuals.
[sec1.p25.s3] Even more important than the average accuracy found is the large degree of accuracy differences between perceivers.
[sec1.p25.s4] Half of the variance in identification accuracy between the 24 perceiver-by-flavor cells sat on the perceiver, with a spread from .12 to .68.
[sec1.p25.s5] This is a stark contrast to findings in interpersonal perception research.
[sec1.p25.s6] Individual perceiver differences in interpersonal accuracy, in the sense of judging what other people are like, are typically small, show only modest generalization across judgment tasks, and emerge particularly when the target is a good one (Rogers & Biesanz, 2019; Schlegel et al., 2017).
[sec1.p25.s7] Where individual differences in accuracy appear, they are explained socially, through motivation, empathic skill, or experience with people, rather than through perceptual skill.
[sec1.p25.s8] Importantly, the pronounced differences between gummy bear eaters were already present from the beginning of the study and did not increase across trials, making differential learning during the tasting sessions an unlikely explanation.
[sec1.p25.s9] Against this background, the findings raise the possibility that gummy bear identification constitutes an individual-difference ability in its own right: Some people may simply be good gummy bear judges, whereas others are not.
[sec1.p25.s10] Whether this ability deserves the status of a genuine gummy bear identification competence would require further evidence that it is reliable across repeated assessments and stable across occasions.
[sec1.p25.s11] An exciting perspective for future research is to identify the process differences underlying this variability in identification accuracy among gummy bear eaters.
[sec1.p25.s12] Potential sources of this

[sec1.p26.s1] PERCEPTION OF GUMMY BEARS 26 variability might include differences in sensory abilities also involved in basic olfactory identification (Doty et al., 1984) as well as social processes including the motivation to get it right with gummy bears, experiences in interacting with gummy bears, and the social identification with certain gummy bear colors.
[sec1.p26.s2] Other open questions regard generalization and consequences of this ability beyond identifying gummy bears.
[sec1.p26.s3] It may be narrowly confined to Goldbears, generalize to other brands or fruit-flavored confectionery, or constitute one manifestation of a broader flavor- or odor-identification ability.
[sec1.p26.s4] Future research might examine whether good gummy bear judges are good judges of other abiotic and living individuals as well and whether this ability is related to intrapersonal, interpersonal, and institutional consequential outcomes (Ozer & Benet-Martínez, 2006).
[sec1.p26.s5] However, whether being a good gummy bear judge is actually beneficial remains open.
[sec1.p26.s6] Research on judgments of humans suggests that greater accuracy does not invariably translate into better outcomes (e.g., Förster et al., 2022).
[sec1.p26.s7] Findings for liking gummy bears show some remarkable resemblance to interpersonal perception research on interpersonal attraction and liking.
[sec1.p26.s8] While stated preferences were consensual and driven by the color/flavor category (participants agreed that raspberry gummy bears are among the tastiest), experienced preferences were specific and driven by the pairing of a gummy bear eater and a flavor (for example, Ella specifically liked pineapple more than expected from her general liking and pineapple's overall popularity).
[sec1.p26.s9] When removing the noise floors and renormalizing, relationship variance for experienced liking was 63% (instead of 37% when including noise) which is in the same range that is found for initial liking (around 50%) and liking in established relationships (close to 70%) of humans, in both romantic and nonromantic (peer) contexts (see Figure 2 in Back et al., 2023).
[sec1.p26.s10] Thus, in the same way that liking humans is particularly due to the specific matching of two human individuals, liking gummy bears is driven by the unique chemistry between a specific gummy

[sec1.p27.s1] PERCEPTION OF GUMMY BEARS 27 bear eater (perceiver) and a specific gummy bear flavor (target).
[sec1.p27.s2] Importantly, this unique chemistry emerged only when the gummy bear was actually encountered: placed in the eater's mouth without its color, flavor name, or reputation available at the time of judgment.
[sec1.p27.s3] Under these conditions, who liked which gummy bear flavor became substantially more individual than the consensual preferences stated beforehand.
[sec1.p27.s4] Regarding awareness of these specific preferences, the same asymmetry is found where the targets are people: stated ideals predict evaluations of hypothetical partners, but not for partners actually met (Eastwick et al., 2014), and show negligible unique fit with realized evaluations (Sparks et al., 2020).
[sec1.p27.s5] Although interacting with gummy bears is arguably less influenced by self-presentation and by prior individual acquaintance, and less personally at stake, the same deviation of judgments appears: Judging gummy bear flavors in an a priori abstract sense deviates from gummy bear evaluations when actually interacting with an individual.
[sec1.p27.s6] Attitude research offers a potential answer: A stated judgment is more driven by categorical information and a tasted one by individual information.
[sec1.p27.s7] Our two measures sit at opposite ends of that continuum by construction (Fiske & Neuberg, 1990): stated liking rated a named flavor aloud, with the label available; tasted liking rated the gummy bear met without it.
[sec1.p27.s8] A consensual category attitude, besides unrelated evaluations of individual members, is the structure LaPiere (1934) reported and the reason the contact literature exists (Allport, 1954).
[sec1.p27.s9] Explicit measures of group attitudes are only weakly related to intergroup behavior toward individuals (Kurdi et al., 2019).
[sec1.p27.s10] A similar dissociation and weak relation were found for the perception of gummy bears.
[sec1.p27.s11] Take the two reds as an example: raspberry and strawberry are near-identical on the product, took the top two stated positions at 5.50 and 5.00 on a 1 to 6 scale, and split in the mouth to third and last of six.
[sec1.p27.s12] Apple showed the reverse pattern.
[sec1.p27.s13] It received a modest stated rating of 3.00, but reached 4.04 when actually tasted, making it one of the two best-tasting flavors.
[sec1.p27.s14] The red gummy bears therefore entered the

[sec1.p28.s1] PERCEPTION OF GUMMY BEARS 28 tasting with a considerable reputational advantage, whereas apple performed better once that reputation was removed from the judgment.
[sec1.p28.s2] The stated liking for the red ones might be an attitude toward a color driven by a learning history (Palmer & Schloss, 2010) that is partly independent of actual taste (e.g., by associating all sorts of positive concepts with the color red).
[sec1.p28.s3] Attitudes built through that history might be a suboptimal predictor of actual conduct toward their object (Glasman & Albarracín, 2006).
[sec1.p28.s4] Our results also speak to the issue of self-knowledge.
[sec1.p28.s5] Self-knowledge has recently been defined as the extent to which people accurately perceive their own relatively stable characteristics and momentary states, explicitly including their preferences (Thielmann et al., 2026).
[sec1.p28.s6] Preferences are an intriguing case because liking is inherently subjective and there is no obvious external observer who should know better than the person herself what she likes.
[sec1.p28.s7] In the present study, repeated liking judgments during blind tasting provided a criterion against which previously stated preferences could be compared.
[sec1.p28.s8] Nevertheless, stated unique preferences only imperfectly corresponded to the unique preferences that emerged during tasting.
[sec1.p28.s9] Thus, even for something as personally accessible as what tastes good in one's own mouth, knowing oneself may not be straightforward.
[sec1.p28.s10] Correspondence also varied descriptively across perceivers, raising the possibility that some gummy bear eaters know their own tastes better than others.
[sec1.p28.s11] With only six flavors per person, however, the present data cannot establish such individual differences.
[sec1.p28.s12] More generally, work on self-knowledge proceeds domain by domain, from traits and feelings to behavior and relationships (Vazire & Wilson, 2012).
[sec1.p28.s13] An important question is how narrowly gummy bear preference selfknowledge is bounded: whether it generalizes to self-knowledge regarding preferences for other foods, and preferences for other abiotic and/or living individuals, or remains specific even within the confectionery domain.
[sec1.p28.s14] A further question is whether people who know more accurately what they like are better able to select and shape environments that fit their actual

[sec1.p29.s1] PERCEPTION OF GUMMY BEARS 29 preferences, an idea that connects to work on self-knowledge and person-environment fit (Rottinghaus & Van Esbroeck, 2011).
[sec1.p29.s2] Given that research on gummy bear perceptions is still in its infancy, and we were only able to provide first results, it is premature to delineate broad practical implications.
[sec1.p29.s3] Nevertheless, one practice these results already bear on is handing gummy bears out.
[sec1.p29.s4] Developmental and behavioral-economic research administers them as rewards by category (Melis et al., 2015), and in one design the reward was selected by the child's stated category preference (Sutter et al., 2015).
[sec1.p29.s5] Our results indicate that most of the systematic variation in a gummy bear's hedonic value lies in the perceiver-flavor pairing, and that a gummy bear eater's stated preference only poorly predicts the experienced liking.
[sec1.p29.s6] The applied literature has measured a similar gap: reinforcer surveys agreed with behavioral assessment on about 57% of items in 20 children (Northup, 2000), and caregiver report matched direct assessment for one toddler in nine (Cote et al., 2007).
[sec1.p29.s7] Items selected by someone else do still function as reinforcers even where their rank ordering is wrong (Cote et al., 2007), a low-preference item sustains responding when it is the only one on offer (Roscoe et al., 1999), and reinforcer selection did not affect performance in typically developing schoolchildren (Resetar & Noell, 2008).
[sec1.p29.s8] These findings make a simple recommendation difficult.
[sec1.p29.s9] If a child states that raspberry is preferred but blind tasting indicates greater liking for apple, giving apple may better match the child's experienced liking while disregarding the child's stated choice.
[sec1.p29.s10] The reinforcing value of a gummy bear may therefore depend not only on how much it is enjoyed when eaten, but also on what is expected, requested, and chosen.
[sec1.p29.s11] Direct assessment of tasted liking should consequently not simply replace stated preference.
[sec1.p29.s12] Rather, future research could examine how stated preference, experienced liking, and choice jointly determine the value of a gummy bear reward.

[sec1.p30.s1] PERCEPTION OF GUMMY BEARS 30 Future research on gummy bear perceptions should first provide well-powered direct replications of the present findings on accuracy, specific preferences, and awareness using a considerably larger and more diverse sample of gummy bear eaters.
[sec1.p30.s2] The person who drew the bear knew its flavor while the test person judged, and a replication should prevent that.
[sec1.p30.s3] A machine drawing the bears from a larger deposit would take the administering person out of the procedure altogether.
[sec1.p30.s4] Such samples would also allow researchers to establish whether the pronounced differences in identification accuracy and the descriptive differences in preference self-knowledge constitute reliable individual differences and to investigate their predictors.
[sec1.p30.s5] The present pattern suggests one particularly interesting possibility.
[sec1.p30.s6] Ella was the best differentiator, the best calibrated about it, and showed the strongest stated-tasted correspondence.
[sec1.p30.s7] Future research could examine whether these characteristics systematically cluster within the same gummy bear eaters.
[sec1.p30.s8] It could also test how narrowly gummy bear identification ability and preference self-knowledge are bounded to the gummy bear domain and whether they generalize to related abilities and forms of self-knowledge.
[sec1.p30.s9] Similarly, a larger sample would allow tests of age differences.
[sec1.p30.s10] In the current data, identification accuracy showed no monotonic pattern with age, even though olfactory identification performance varies systematically with age, with adults performing better than children and older adults (Doty et al., 1984; Oleszkiewicz et al., 2019), and children rely more strongly on color cues in flavor identification (Oram et al., 1995).
[sec1.p30.s11] A larger sample spanning different ages could test these developmental differences while also separating age from differences in prior experience with gummy bears.
[sec1.p30.s12] Second, variations of the design could be used to investigate the role of color and a priori attitudes toward gummy bears more directly (e.g., between- or within-individual variation of color-visible vs. color blind liking judgments), and analyze the role of motivational factors for accurate identification (e.g., providing different sorts of social or material rewards).
[sec1.p30.s13] The

[sec1.p31.s1] PERCEPTION OF GUMMY BEARS 31 availability of category information could also be manipulated more systematically by comparing blind tasting with conditions in which the flavor identity is announced before tasting or the gummy bear is seen before it is eaten.
[sec1.p31.s2] Such designs would allow direct tests of whether the discrepancy between stated and tasted liking is partly produced by knowing the gummy bear's category and whether the experience of the same flavor changes when its identity is known.
[sec1.p31.s3] Similarly, manipulating incentives for correct identification could help establish whether good gummy bear judging reflects primarily sensory discrimination or also the motivation to use the available sensory information accurately.
[sec1.p31.s4] Third, future research could examine what happens when gummy bear eaters are confronted with discrepancies between their stated preferences and their own blind tasting data.
[sec1.p31.s5] After completing the tasting task, participants could receive feedback showing how their initial preference ratings corresponded to the liking ratings they gave when flavor identity was unavailable.
[sec1.p31.s6] Their preferences and subsequent choices could then be assessed again.
[sec1.p31.s7] This would allow researchers to examine whether people revise their prior beliefs in light of their own experienced liking, retain their original preferences, or change their interpretation of the tasting experience instead.
[sec1.p31.s8] Of particular interest would be individual differences in such belief updating.
[sec1.p31.s9] Some gummy bear eaters might readily incorporate discrepant evidence into their self-knowledge, whereas others might continue to prefer the gummy bears they believed they preferred before seeing the data.
[sec1.p31.s10] This extension connects the present findings to broader questions about the changeability of self-knowledge and the role of feedback in correcting or maintaining self-perceptions.
[sec1.p31.s11] Finally, the present study illustrates how established approaches from sensory research can be combined with methods from interpersonal perception to study individual differences in the perception and evaluation of abiotic targets.
[sec1.p31.s12] Gummy bears provide a particularly convenient model system because they are highly standardized and reproducible, belong to familiar

[sec1.p32.s1] PERCEPTION OF GUMMY BEARS 32 categories whose identity cues can be made available or concealed, and allow repeated encounters with exemplars of the same target category.
[sec1.p32.s2] This combination permits researchers to examine not only how category information shapes perception and liking, but also how much these processes differ between perceivers, how well perceivers know their own idiosyncratic responses, and how they respond when subsequent evidence conflicts with their prior beliefs.
[sec1.p32.s3] It is an exciting open question for future research whether these processes operate similarly for the multitude of further abiotic individuals, such as plush toys, dolls, paintings, even personalized stones, pens, and so forth, we deal with, perceive, and evaluate in our everyday lives.
